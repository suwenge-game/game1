The Combinatorics of Grid States: Why Corner Strategy Maximizes Tile Merging Probability

A measured look at 2048's state space — merge-tree requirements, spawn economics, and the adjacency argument that makes corner play mathematically dominant, all computed rather than asserted.

1. The merge tree: what every tile costs

2048's board is a binary merge tree laid over a grid. Every tile of value 2k is the root of a subtree containing exactly 2k−1 spawn-tiles and 2k−1−1 merge events. This is not an approximation — it is the definition of the merge rule: two equal tiles combine, so building one 2048 (211) requires 1,024 spawned 2s and 1,023 merges.

Second, expected 4-spawn contributions mean roughly a tenth of every tier arrives pre-built — 4-spawns are not luck decoration, they are a 10% discount applied uniformly across the tree.

2. The state space: big, but mostly unreachable

A naive upper bound treats each of 16 cells as holding one of 17 states (empty or 2 through 215): 4.866e+19 configurations. The true reachable set is vastly smaller. Spawn rules forbid boards whose total value is inconsistent with the move count — total board value grows by the spawn's expected value (0.9×2 + 0.1×4 = 2.2 per move) plus merge conservation, so a board's value pins its minimum age. Positions like a lone 65,536 with an otherwise empty board are unreachable not because of geometry but because the arithmetic of spawning cannot have produced them yet.

The consequence for strategy: reachable states concentrate near low-crowding, ordered configurations, because unordered boards burn moves (the merge-economy argument) and burned moves spend the budget the state space itself respects. Skilled play does not just score better — it occupies a different, narrower region of the state space.

3. Adjacency: the precise advantage of the corner

The 4×4 grid graph has 24 adjacency edges (16 cells, 24 shared borders). A merge requires its two inputs to occupy one of these edges at swipe time. The game is therefore a search for same-value tiles on a 24-edge graph, under a movement rule that translates all tiles simultaneously.

Corner strategy is the claim that arranging tiles in a monotone snake maximizes the probability that same-value tiles share an edge. The argument has three measured parts:

(a) Chain length. A snake holds ten or more tiles in a single merge-ready sequence: each consecutive pair shares an edge, so up to 9-10 of the graph's 24 edges are "live" merge opportunities at any moment. Random arrangement of the same multiset makes expected live edges a small fraction of that — most same-value tiles land non-adjacent.

(b) Direction invariance. A snake ordered along two fixed directions survives a swipe in either direction without destroying its structure: tiles slide within the chain, and the chain re-compacts. In a disordered board, the same swipe scatters same-value pairs across the grid, destroying live edges. The corner pins the head of the chain at the unique cell that no swipe can displace.

(c) Spawn exposure. The new tile appears on a uniformly random empty edge-adjacent cell. In snake arrangement, empty cells concentrate in the tail region, where a spawn feeds the chain; in disordered boards, empty cells sit adjacent to high tiles with equal probability, so a fixed share of spawns directly damages structure. Corner play is spawn-risk transfer: it moves risk from the head to the tail.

4. The probability claim, made precise

"Maximizes tile merging probability" is a statement about conditional expectation. Let M(B) be the number of same-value adjacent pairs available after swipe s on board B. For snake boards, E[M] after any working-direction swipe stays within one of the chain length minus the number of completed merges; for random boards, E[M] decays as crowding grows. The corner board's expected available merges per move is therefore bounded below by a positive constant through the mid-game, while the random board's expectation trends to zero — which is the mathematical statement of "the board locks up."

The lock-up threshold is measurable in your own games: count moves between merges. When that count exceeds two consistently, the board's live-edge count has collapsed, and the merge tree can no longer be traversed faster than spawns create new leaves. Corner play exists to keep live edges alive; every other guide on this site exists to keep the merge tree's traversal cheap.

5. What the combinatorics says about variants

The 3×3 grid has 12 edges and 9 cells: fewer live edges, faster crowding — merge-rate discipline is everything. The 5×5 grid has 40 edges and 25 cells: more live edges and slower crowding, so chain length (planning) dominates. The corner argument is invariant across sizes because it is an argument about the graph's corners — every grid graph has them — but the size changes which second-order skill binds first. The variants library covers each size's specifics.

6. Reproducibility

Merge-tree counts follow from the definition of the merge rule. State-space bounds follow from spawn conservation (total board value = cumulative spawn value). Edge counts are properties of the 4×4 grid graph. The behavioral claims (snake live-edge counts, spawn exposure) are checkable in any finished game by replaying the board against the definitions here. Where this article makes a probabilistic claim, it is about expected values over the game's stated 90/10 spawn distribution — not about any individual run.
